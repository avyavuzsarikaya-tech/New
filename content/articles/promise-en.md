Every economy is, at bottom, a web of promises. The employer promises a wage. The buyer promises to pay. The seller promises that the goods are what they claim to be. When those promises hold, strangers can trade with one another across great distances.

When they fail, the damage is rarely shared evenly. A broken promise costs the powerful an inconvenience; it can cost a small trader a livelihood. That is why so many traditions treated honesty in trade not as good manners but as a matter of justice.

The Universal Declaration affirms that everyone who works has the right to just and favourable remuneration.[^1] The International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights goes further, recognising the right of everyone to an adequate standard of living and to the continuous improvement of living conditions.[^2]

> Wealth earned by taking what belongs to another is not wealth. It is a debt that has not yet been called.

None of this requires a single economic system. It requires something simpler and harder: that the rules of exchange do not quietly favour the stronger party. Clear contracts, honest measures and debts that do not grow beyond what any honest borrower could repay are old ideas. They are worth rediscovering.

An economy that keeps its promises is not only fairer. It is more stable, because people who trust the rules are willing to build within them.
