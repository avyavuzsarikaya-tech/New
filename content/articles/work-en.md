There is an old instruction, found in more than one tradition, to pay the worker before their sweat has dried. It is a striking image. It asks the employer to see the effort behind the invoice, and to treat delay itself as a kind of injustice.

Modern law says something similar in a more formal voice. The Universal Declaration recognises not only the right to work and to just pay, but also the right to rest and leisure, including reasonable limits on working hours.[^1]

The International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights adds safe and healthy working conditions and equal opportunity for promotion.[^2] Taken together, these texts describe work as a relationship between people, not a transaction between a buyer and a commodity.

> A wage that arrives late has already lost part of its value. So has the trust behind it.

Today the forms of work are changing quickly. Many people now work through platforms, on short contracts or across borders. The questions, however, are the old ones. Is the pay fair and on time? Is there time to rest? Is the work safe? Is the worker treated with dignity?

A society can be judged by how it answers. The answers do not depend on technology. They depend on whether we still believe that the person doing the work matters as much as the work itself.
