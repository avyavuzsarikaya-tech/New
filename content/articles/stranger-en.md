Every court in the world is fair to the people it already respects. The harder test is the person who arrives with nothing: no language in common with the judge, no family name that opens doors, no money for a skilled advocate.

The Universal Declaration begins its list of rights with a simple claim: all human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights.[^1] It goes on to say that everyone is entitled to equal protection of the law, without discrimination. These are not rewards for good citizenship. They belong to a person simply because they are a person.

The founders of the United Nations wrote in its Charter of their determination to reaffirm faith in the dignity and worth of the human person.[^2] The word they chose was faith. They understood that law alone cannot create respect for others; it can only protect a respect that already exists.

For anyone who has practised law, the lesson is familiar. Rules are only as just as the hands that apply them. A procedure that is perfectly fair on paper can still fail the stranger if no one takes the trouble to explain it.

> Justice that must be purchased is not justice. It is merchandise.

This is why legal aid, translation and patient explanation are not luxuries added to a justice system. They are the system. Where they are missing, the law begins to serve only those who already understand it.
