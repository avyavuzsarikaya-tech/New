The day a war ends is rarely the day peace begins. Weapons can fall silent in an afternoon. The trust that made ordinary life possible takes far longer to return.

The Charter of the United Nations opens with the determination to save succeeding generations from the scourge of war.[^1] It was written by people who had lived through two world wars and knew that victory alone does not secure peace.

What does secure it? Experience suggests a few things. People need to see that wrongs will be addressed by law rather than by revenge. Families need to know what happened to those they lost. Property and land need to be returned or fairly compensated. And the young need a reason to believe that the future will be better than the past.

> Peace is not the absence of an enemy. It is the presence of justice.

The Universal Declaration warns that if people are not to be compelled, as a last resort, to rebellion against tyranny and oppression, human rights must be protected by the rule of law.[^2] Read after a war, the sentence is a practical instruction.

Rebuilding is slow, and it rarely makes headlines. But it is where peace is really won or lost.
