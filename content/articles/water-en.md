A river does not ask who owns it. It rises in one country, waters the fields of a second and reaches the sea through a third. Long before there were states, people understood that water could be used but never truly possessed. It was held in common, and those who drew from it carried a duty to those downstream.

In 2010 the United Nations General Assembly recognised safe and clean drinking water and sanitation as a human right.[^1] The resolution did not invent a new idea. It put into the language of modern law something that many religious and legal traditions had long taught: that no one may be denied what they need to live.

> To waste water is to spend something that was never only ours.

The difficulty is not that we lack principles. It is that the principles are easy to agree with and hard to keep. The world's governments have committed themselves to ensuring water and sanitation for all,[^2] yet the distance between a signed commitment and a working tap in a village can be very long.

What would it mean to treat water as a trust again? It would mean measuring success not by how much we can draw, but by what we leave behind. It would mean that the farmer upstream and the family downstream are understood as neighbours, not competitors.

The Universal Declaration speaks of a standard of living adequate for health and well-being.[^3] There is no such standard without water. That is where the common good begins.
